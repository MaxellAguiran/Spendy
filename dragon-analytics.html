First things first

What does "predictive analytics" actually mean?

In one sentence

It means using the things that already happened in your business to make a good guess about what will happen next.

Here is a simple example. You look at all the customers who left you last year. You notice most of them stopped opening your emails about two months before they cancelled. Now, when a current customer stops opening emails, you know there is a real risk they will leave. So you can call them first.

That is all it is. Looking at the past carefully, so you are not surprised by the future. The maths behind it can get complicated, but you never need to understand the maths. That is my job. Your job is to know your business, and to tell me what decision you need help with.

I work directly with the person who is responsible for the number — the person running the ad budget, or the owner watching customers leave. We take one question at a time. Projects are small and clear, they take weeks rather than months, and you always know the price before we start.

Two words you will see a lot

ROAS means "return on ad spend". It answers: for every $1 I spend on ads, how many dollars come back? If you spend $1 and get $4 back, your ROAS is 4.

Churn means customers who stop paying you and go away. If 5 of your 100 clients leave this month, your churn is 5%.

Example 1 · An advertising agency

Moving the ad budget to the places that actually bring sales

The problem

An agency spends money on search ads, social ads and display ads for its shop clients. The split between them has not really changed in a year. Their reports give all the credit to the last ad a customer clicked, which makes search look great and social look useless — even though social is often what made the customer interested in the first place. The client keeps asking: "if we move $50,000, what happens?" Nobody can answer.

What I would do
  • Put the ad data and the real sales data together in one place
  • Build a model that measures what each channel adds, including the slow effect of ads people saw but did not click
  • Work out what the next dollar earns in each channel, not the average dollar
  • Test the model against past months to check it would have been right
  • Hand over a simple tool: type in a budget, see the suggested split and the likely result
What the agency ends up with
  • A clear list of where money is being wasted
  • A suggested budget split, with an honest range instead of a fake exact number
  • A confident answer for the monthly client meeting
  • A model they can re-run themselves as new data arrives
How we would judge it

Before starting, we agree on the numbers below. After the new budget has been running long enough, we check them together.

Example 2 · A marketing firm

Knowing which clients are about to leave, while there is still time to help

The problem

A firm has clients who pay every month. They only learn a client is unhappy when the cancellation email arrives. The team gives the same amount of attention to every client, so the ones quietly getting ready to leave get no extra help. Money slips away and nobody sees the pattern until it is too late.

What I would do
  • Gather each client's history: how long they have stayed, changes to their bill, how often they talk to your team, and the results you delivered
  • Agree exactly what "leaving" means and how early we want to know
  • Build a model that gives every client a risk score, and test it on clients it has never seen before
  • Sort the list by money at risk, not just risk — so effort goes where it matters
  • Show the reasons behind each score, so your team knows what to actually do
What the firm ends up with
  • A monthly list of clients, most valuable and most at risk first
  • The two or three reasons behind each client's score
  • A simple plan for the most common warning signs
  • An estimate of what each saved client is worth
How we would judge it

Same as above: we agree the measures first, then check them honestly once the model has been used for a while.

Common questions

Things people ask me before we start.

English is not my first language. Is that a problem?

Not at all, and please do not worry about it. I write and speak in simple English on purpose. If I ever use a word you do not know, just ask and I will say it a different way. I would much rather explain something twice than have you nod politely and stay confused.

Do I need to understand data science?

No. You need to understand your business — that is the hard part, and you already do it. I handle everything technical and explain the results in normal words.

What data do I need to have?

Usually less than people expect. A spreadsheet export, or access to the tools you already use, is often enough to begin. If it is not enough, I will tell you early rather than take your money and struggle.

How long does a project take?

Most projects take between three and six weeks from our first call to you having something you can use. Small questions can be much faster.

How much does it cost?

It depends on the size of the question, so I give you a fixed price before any work starts. You will never get a surprise bill. The first call is always free.

What if you cannot help me?

Then I will say so, quickly and clearly, and I will not charge you for finding out. If I know someone better suited to your problem, I will point you to them.

Is my business information safe?

Yes. I only ask for the data needed to answer your question, I do not share it with anyone, and I am happy to sign a confidentiality agreement before you send me anything.
