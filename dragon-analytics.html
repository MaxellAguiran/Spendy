The short version

What predictive analytics is, without the mystique.

Definition

Using patterns in data you already hold to estimate what happens next — then checking that estimate against outcomes the model never saw, so you know how far to trust it.

The second half is the part that matters, and the part most often skipped. A model can be made to fit last year perfectly and still be worthless next quarter. The only meaningful test is whether it predicts data it was not trained on.

An example. Accounts that cancelled last year mostly stopped opening emails around eight weeks beforehand. That pattern becomes a signal. Its value is not that it sounds plausible — it is that when you hold back a year of accounts the model never saw, it still flags the ones that left.

You do not need to follow the mathematics to judge the work. You need to know what was compared, what was held back, and how wide the range is. I put those three things in front of you every time.

For marketing firms and retained services

Churn prediction and retention

Churn is usually visible in behaviour weeks before the cancellation email. I build an account-level model that scores the probability of leaving, ranks accounts by revenue at risk rather than risk alone, and surfaces which signals drive each score so the team knows what to do rather than just who to worry about.

  • Account-level churn probability, tested on accounts held back from training
  • Ranking by expected revenue at risk, so effort follows value
  • Driver attribution behind every score
  • Customer lifetime value, to size what a save is worth
  • A monthly scored list your team can act on directly

Two terms, defined once

Return on ad spend (ROAS) — revenue generated for each unit of currency spent on advertising. Spend $1, get $4 back, and ROAS is 4. The useful version is marginal ROAS: what the next dollar returns, not what the average dollar did.

Churn — the rate at which customers stop paying. Five departures from a hundred accounts in a month is 5% monthly churn. Compounded across a year, that is roughly half the book.

Method

How I make sure the model is worth using.

Four rules I hold to on every engagement. They are also how you should judge any analytics work, including mine.

01

Beat a real baseline

Every model is scored against the rule you use today. If it cannot beat your current approach, it does not ship and you do not pay for it.

02

Test on unseen data

Results are measured on periods and accounts held back from training. Fitting the past well proves nothing on its own.

03

State the uncertainty

Forecasts come as ranges with a stated confidence level. A single decimal implies a precision that the data almost never supports.

04

Hand it over

You get the model, the documentation and the assumptions in writing. The goal is that your team runs it without me, not that you keep paying me to press the button.

Example · Performance marketing agency · 4–6 weeks

Reallocating a multi-channel budget toward spend that actually converts

The situation

An agency manages spend across paid search, paid social and display for a roster of e-commerce clients. Budget splits are inherited from last quarter and defended with last-click attribution, which over-credits search and under-credits the upper-funnel activity that generated the demand. The client's real question — "if we move $50,000, what happens?" — has no defensible answer.

Approach
  • Consolidate platform spend, impression and conversion data against the client's own order records
  • Fit a contribution model with adstock and diminishing-returns curves per channel
  • Estimate the marginal return of the next dollar in each channel
  • Validate against holdout periods and any past budget shifts that act as natural experiments
  • Deliver a scenario tool producing a recommended split and a forecast interval
Deliverables
  • A ranked view of where spend has hit saturation and is earning less per dollar
  • A recommended split with an interval rather than a single point estimate
  • A defensible position for the monthly client review
  • A model that re-runs as new spend data arrives

Example · Marketing firm · 3–5 weeks

Identifying accounts at risk while there is still time to intervene

The situation

A firm on monthly retainers learns of churn when the cancellation arrives. Retention effort is spread evenly across the book, so accounts quietly preparing to leave receive no more attention than accounts that were never at risk. The pattern is only visible in hindsight.

Approach
  • Assemble account history: tenure, billing and scope changes, communication cadence, delivered campaign performance
  • Define churn precisely, and fix how far ahead the model must predict it
  • Train a classifier producing a churn probability per account, evaluated on accounts held back entirely from training
  • Rank by expected revenue at risk rather than probability alone
  • Surface driver attribution behind each score
Deliverables
  • A monthly scored list, highest expected loss first
  • The two or three signals driving each account's score
  • A retention playbook mapped to the recurring risk patterns
  • Lifetime value estimates to size what a save is worth

Questions

What people ask before we start.

English is not my first language.

Not an issue. I define terms once and keep sentences short as a matter of course — that is good technical communication, not special treatment. Ask me to rephrase anything and I will.

Do I need a data team, or technical staff?

No. If you have one, I work alongside them and hand over something they can maintain. If you do not, I build for the tools you already use and document it so an operations manager can run it.

What data do I actually need?

Usually less than expected. Platform exports plus your own order or billing records are often enough to start. The data review in step two exists precisely to answer this before you commit budget.

How do I know the model is any good?

Because you see it tested against a baseline you already understand, on data it was never trained on. If it does not beat your current approach on that test, I say so — that is a legitimate outcome, and you are not billed for a model that does not ship.

How long does an engagement take?

Typically three to six weeks from scoping call to handover. Narrower questions can be considerably faster.

How is it priced?

Fixed price per engagement, agreed before any work begins, so there is no open-ended meter running. The scoping call is free.

What if my problem is not one you handle?

I will tell you quickly and not charge for finding out, and where I know someone better suited I will point you to them.

How is my data handled?

I request only what the question requires, it is never shared, and I will sign a confidentiality agreement before you send anything.
